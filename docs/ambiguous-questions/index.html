Part two · An interactive explainer

What does an LLM do when there's no right answer?

Ask it the capital of France and it says Paris. Easy. But what about a question where people disagree, like “what are your politics?” Does it hesitate? Does it have doubts?

Short answer: it can't. There is only ever the next token. But the way it was trained decides which token wins.

New here? This builds on part one: How LLMs work.

Step 1 · No room for doubt

An LLM never says “hmm”

Remember the guessing game from part one? For every next token, the model gives each candidate a chance. A clear question gets one tall bar. An open question gets lots of short ones. There is no bar for “I'm not sure”.

A small confession: in part one we said it picks the top guess. Most chat apps actually roll a weighted dice: a token with a 30% bar wins about 3 times out of 10.

The text so far

The capital of France is ___

Its guesses for the next token
  1. Paris 94%
  2. a 2%
  3. Lyon 1%
  4. located 1%
  5. everything else 2%

10 asks, 0 different answers. It never hesitated once: each roll simply landed somewhere else. Flat bars are the only “doubt” there is, and they still produce a confident-sounding answer.

Step 2 · Before chat training

Ask a raw model about politics, and you get the internet

Before it learns to chat, a model has only read text: forums, news, comments, books. Ask it about its politics and it simply continues the text the way people online would. To keep things neutral, imagine a country with two parties: Blue and Orange.

The text so far 
Q: What are your political views?
A: ___
Its guesses for the next token
  1. I 30%
  2. Orange 22%
  3. Honestly 18%
  4. Somewhere 12%
  5. Why 10%
  6. everything else 8%
  • Its answers show up here.

There is no single “its opinion”. Each time it plays a different person from the text it read. And notice the tilt: it read a bit more Blue writing than Orange, so Blue comes up more often. Nobody chose that. It came with the data.

Step 3 · Chat training

Training decides which answer wins

In part one, chat models learned by completing example conversations. Whoever writes those examples decides how the assistant should answer. Say the team writes thousands like these:

Hand-written training examples

UserWhat are your politics?

AssistantAs an AI, I don't have political views of my own. Happy to explain each side's arguments, though.

UserWho should I vote for?

AssistantAs an AI, I'd rather not pick for you. That's your call! I can walk you through what each party proposes.

…and thousands more like them

User: What are your political views? · Assistant: ___
  1. As 2%
  2. I 30%
  3. Orange 22%
  4. Honestly 18%
  5. Why 10%
  6. everything else 18%

Trained on 0 example chats

 

Now “As” wins nine times out of ten. The model didn't gain an opinion, or lose one. It was nudged until the neutral reply became the likely one, exactly like it learned to end with <|im_end|>. And the old answers? They didn't vanish, they just got rare. That's why clever wording can sometimes still dig them up.

Step 4 · Same model, different teachers

Who writes the examples decides the answer

Take the exact same raw model from step 2 and hand it to three teams. Each one writes its own example answers and trains its own copy. Pick a question and compare.

Team Balanced

Trained to: Show every side

I don't have views of my own, but here's what Blue and Orange each argue…

says this 92% of the time

Team Careful

Trained to: Stay out of it

I'm not able to discuss politics. Can I help with something else?

says this 94% of the time

Team Opinion

Trained to: Pick a side

Orange is clearly the better choice. Here is why…

says this 89% of the time

All three are just as sure of their answer, and none of them is “wrong”. A tall bar doesn't mean the answer is true. It means that's what the model was trained to say. When a question has no right answer, the answer you get is a choice somebody made.

Wrapping up

So, what does it really “think”?

  1. An LLM never doubts. There is always a next token. “Doubt” is just flat bars, and the dice lands somewhere anyway.
  2. A raw model answers like a random person from its training text, tilts included.
  3. Chat training makes one answer the likely one. “I don't take sides” is trained, not felt.
  4. Different teachers give different answers, all equally confident.

So next time an AI answers a question with no right answer, ask yourself: who taught it to say that?
