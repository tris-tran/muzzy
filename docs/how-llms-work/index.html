How does an LLM actually work?

You type a question, and a few seconds later a machine writes back. What happens in between? Let's find out, one small step at a time.

Step 1 · The view from outside

It starts as a black box

From the outside, an LLM is surprisingly simple: you give it some words, and it gives you some words back. Pick a question and send it in.

Step 2 · You've seen one already

Every time you Google something

Search on Google these days and, above the usual links, there's often an AI Overview: a short answer written just for you.

Nobody wrote that answer in advance. It's written on the spot by Gemini, Google's LLM, which reads what the search found and sums it up in its own words. Your search goes in, Gemini's words come out, one at a time. The same black box.

So what is actually going on inside the box?

Step 3 · One word at a time

Why does it come out word by word?

Because that's really how it works.

An LLM can only do one thing: look at some text and guess what comes next. It's the same game as finishing a familiar sentence. You can do it too, without even trying:

Each run of the model is called an inference, and each run adds exactly one piece, a token (usually a word, sometimes part of one, or a full stop). Want the next one? Run it again.

Step 4 · Learning to guess

So how does it get good at guessing?

Nobody teaches it grammar or facts. It plays that same finish-the-sentence game, over and over, on real text: books, websites, articles. We hide the next word, let it guess, then check. Guess wrong and it gets punished. Guess right and it gets rewarded.

Step 5 · Opening the box

Those dials have a real name: weights

Open up the black box and you find a web of connections. Each line is a weight: just a number. Your words are turned into numbers, those numbers flow through the web, getting multiplied by every weight on the way, and out comes a score for each possible next word.

Step 6 · Why so many?

The size of an LLM is its number of weights

Everything a model has picked up, grammar, facts, style, jokes, lives in its weights. No single weight “knows” that Paris is in France; it's spread thinly across millions of them. More weights means more room to learn. That's why, when people say a model is “7B”, they mean 7 billion weights.

175 billion

weights · GPT-3

If you counted one per second, non-stop about 5,500 years longer than writing has existed
Just to store them about 350 GB more than most phones can hold

And here's the catch: to write each single token, the model runs your text through all of its weights, once. Hundreds of billions of multiplications, for every word. That's why LLMs need huge, power-hungry computers, and part of why the answer trickles out word by word.

Step 8 · Training, one last time

Chat models learn by completing whole replies

Remember the guessing game from step 4? To make a chat assistant, the training text is millions of example conversations in this exact format. The assistant's part is hidden, and the model has to guess it piece by piece, all the way to <|im_end|>.

The whole story

  1. From the outside, an LLM is a black box: text in, text out.
  2. Inside, it only ever guesses the next piece of text, one at a time.
  3. It learned to guess by playing that game on huge amounts of text, getting nudged after every guess.
  4. What gets nudged are its weights: just numbers, billions of them.
  5. A chat is the same trick: the whole conversation goes in, every time, and it completes the assistant's turn.
