How do you make a file smaller?

Every photo you send, every song you stream, even this page: they all travel squeezed. How do you shrink something without losing it? And when is it fine to lose a little?

On the way: the big picture, how gz squeezes text (and anything else), how JPEG squeezes photos, and why MP3 is the same idea for your ears.

Step 2 · Inside the compress machine

Write repeats once, in a dictionary

Let's open the machine. It reads the whole text and counts the words. The ones that keep coming back get a tiny code, and that list goes at the top of the new file. Then the text is copied in, with codes instead of words.

Step 3 · When it doesn't work

Sometimes it comes out bigger

Every gz file starts with a small label, about 20 bytes, that says how to unpack it. A bit like our dictionary header. If the text is tiny, or nothing in it repeats, there's nothing to win back, and the "compressed" file ends up bigger.

Step 5 · Losing a little, on purpose

If you know what it is, you can throw some away

Step 6 · Inside JPEG

How JPEG squeezes a photo

JPEG is made just for photos. It knows how eyes work, and uses that to throw away what you won't miss. It does it in six stages. Over the next pages we'll follow one tiny piece of this sunset through all of them.

Only two stages actually throw something away. The others just rewrite the picture in a smarter way, so that the throwing away can happen where you won't notice it.

Inside JPEG · stage 1 of 6

Brightness first, colour second

A screen makes every pixel from red, green and blue light. JPEG starts by describing each pixel differently: how bright it is, plus what colour it is.

Getting the picture ready…

Why? Because of how eyes are built. We have far more cells for light and dark than for colour. We notice a sharp edge in brightness straight away, but we're bad at seeing exactly where one colour stops and the next begins.

So JPEG keeps less colour. Brightness stays at full detail, but colour gets just one value for every 2 × 2 block of pixels: a quarter of the colour detail. Up close you can see the blocks; put back together, it still looks sharp, because the brightness carries the edges.

Half the data, gone already. Before anything clever happens, the picture is half the size. This is the first of the two stages that lose something.

Inside JPEG · stage 2 of 6

Cut it into tiles of 8 × 8 pixels

Next, the picture is cut into a grid of small tiles, 8 pixels wide and 8 tall. From here on, each tile is squeezed on its own, without looking at its neighbours.

Getting the picture ready…

Why so small? Inside a tiny tile, things don't usually change much: a patch of sky, a bit of an edge. That makes every tile simple to describe, and that's what the next stage takes advantage of.

Our tile is where a hill meets the bright sky: light at the top, dark at the bottom, with an edge running through it. It's one of the busiest tiles in the picture, which makes it a good one to follow.

To the computer, it's just 64 numbers. The colour layers are cut into tiles too, and go through exactly the same stages. To keep things simple, we'll only follow this brightness tile.

Inside JPEG · stage 3 of 6 · part 1 of 3

Any row of pixels is a mix of waves

This is the clever part of JPEG, so let's go slowly. Forget the whole tile for a moment and take just one row of it: 8 pixels, from dark to light. Can we describe it a smarter way?

Getting the picture ready…

8 standard waves. The first is flat: it just sets the average brightness. The next goes from one side to the other once, and each one after that wiggles a bit faster. Mix them in the right amounts, and you can make any row of 8 pixels. Exactly.

So a row can be written as 8 amounts instead of 8 pixels. Same number of numbers, nothing lost. A negative amount just means the wave is flipped upside down.

Look at the amounts. The first few waves are big and do almost all the work. The fast wiggles add very little. In photos that's nearly always true, because neighbouring pixels tend to be similar. Remember that: it's the whole trick.

Inside JPEG · stage 3 of 6 · part 2 of 3

From 8 waves to 64 patterns

A tile isn't one row, it's 8 rows and 8 columns. So JPEG crosses the waves: one going across, one going down. 8 waves across × 8 waves down makes 64 patterns.

× =

Wave 2 across, crossed with wave 1 down: a checkerboard.

Reading the chart. Top left is flat. Along the top row the stripes stand up (waves going across); down the left side they lie flat (waves going down). Everything in between is a mix of both, and the further towards the bottom right, the finer and busier the pattern.

Any tile at all can be made by mixing these 64 patterns in the right amounts, just like the row was made from 8 waves. A sky, an eye, a letter: anything.

They're always the same 64. Every JPEG ever made uses exactly these patterns, and every phone and computer already knows them. So they're never sent. Only the amounts are.

Inside JPEG · stage 3 of 6 · part 3 of 3

Our tile, written as a recipe

Back to our tile. JPEG works out how much of each of the 64 patterns it needs. That list of 64 amounts is the recipe, and it's stored instead of the pixels.

Getting the picture ready…

Reading the recipe. It's laid out exactly like the pattern chart: the number in each spot says how much of the pattern in the same spot. Blue means add it, red means add it flipped. The stronger the colour, the bigger the amount. Point at any number to see its pattern.

Big numbers top left, tiny ones bottom right. Our tile is mostly “light on top, dark below”, which is what the smooth patterns describe. The busy patterns only add the fine grain.

Still nothing lost. Mix all 64 and you get our tile back, pixel for pixel. It's the same tile, just written differently. But now the important part and the unimportant part are separated. And that's what the next stage uses.

Inside JPEG · stage 4 of 6

Round the recipe off

Here's where JPEG really throws things away. Every amount in the recipe is divided by a number from a table, and rounded to a whole number. Lots of them become 0.

Getting the picture ready…

Dividing and rounding keeps an amount roughly, in big steps. Divide by 1 and you keep it exactly. Divide by 100, and anything smaller than 50 becomes 0. Point at any number to see its sum.

The table is the clever bit. It's part of the JPEG standard: small dividers for the smooth patterns (top left), which you'd notice, and big ones for the busy patterns (bottom right), which eyes barely see. So fine detail is the first thing to go.

Quality just scales the table. Lower quality means bigger dividers, more zeros, a smaller file and a rougher picture. Try 10, then 90, and watch the zeros and the tile.

Inside JPEG · stage 5 of 6

Pack the numbers tightly

After rounding, the recipe is mostly zeros. The last stage writes it down in as little space as possible, and this part loses nothing.

Getting the picture ready…

1. Zigzag. The numbers are read in a zigzag, from the smooth patterns to the busy ones. The busy ones were mostly rounded to 0, so this way all the zeros pile up at the end.

2. “End”. Instead of writing out all those zeros, JPEG writes one “End” marker, meaning “the rest are all zeros”. A plain bit of sky ends up as almost nothing: its brightness, maybe a number or two, and End.

3. Short codes. Finally everything is written with codes where the most common things (small numbers, runs of zeros) get the shortest ones. The same idea as gz and our dictionary from before: nothing lost, just written smarter.

Inside JPEG · stage 6 of 6

Unpack it, and compare

When you open the photo, your phone or computer runs every stage backwards. The picture comes back very close, but not exactly: what was rounded away is gone for good.

Getting the picture ready…

A real one. The picture on the right is an actual JPEG your browser just made, at the quality you picked. Around 50 to 80 it looks the same and is a tiny fraction of the size. Drag it down to 5 and you'll see the 8 × 8 tiles.

Saving again makes it worse. Every time a JPEG is edited and saved, the rounding happens again on the already-rounded picture. That's why photos shared over and over end up looking blotchy.

The same idea, everywhere. Know what kind of data it is, know what people notice, and throw away the rest. Photos, video, and, as we'll see, sound.

The end · There are lots of them

Two families, hundreds of methods

gz and JPEG are just two of them. Every kind of data has its own methods, and they all fall into one of the two families: those that give back everything, and those that throw away what you won't notice. Point at a name to see what it's for.

Lossless Nothing lost: text, files, code, exact pictures

ZIP The classic: a whole folder packed into one file. Inside, it uses the same method as gzip.

Lossy A little lost on purpose: photos, music, video

MP3 Songs, without the sounds ears can’t hear. More on it below.

Video · how YouTube sends one

About 30 pictures a second: unsqueezed, one second of HD is 186 MB. YouTube sends about half a MB. Each picture is squeezed much like JPEG, but most aren't sent whole: only what moved since the last one. It also checks your internet every few seconds and sends a sharper or rougher version to keep it playing.

Sound · MP3

A minute of CD music is 10 MB; as MP3, about 1 MB. JPEG's idea for ears: turn each tiny slice of song into "how much of each pitch", and drop what you can't hear: too high, too quiet, or hidden behind a louder sound (striped).
