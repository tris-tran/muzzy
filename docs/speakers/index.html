How does a speaker make sound?

Phones, headphones, the Bluetooth speaker on your desk: they all do what a wind-up machine did 130 years ago. To understand them, let's start with that machine.

On the way: a bit of history, very old recordings, how a gramophone works, how electricity turned it into a speaker, what sound files like WAV and MP3 are, radio, and finally Bluetooth.

Step 1 · A bit of history

130 years of making music loud

The shapes changed a lot, all the way to today's Bluetooth speakers. The idea underneath barely changed at all, and it starts with a machine that didn't even need electricity.

Step 2 · Listen

What it sounded like

Real recordings, the oldest from 1888. Until 1925 there was no electricity anywhere: a horn caught the sound, and a needle cut it into wax.

  2. 1911 Una furtiva lagrima · Enrico Caruso Acoustic · disc
  3. 1917 Livery Stable Blues · Original Dixieland Jass Band Acoustic · disc
  4. Jan 1925 St. Louis Blues · Bessie Smith and Louis Armstrong Acoustic · disc
  5. Feb 1925 You May Be Lonesome · Art Gillham Electrical · microphone

Try this: play the last two. They were recorded only weeks apart, but the second one used one of the first microphones. Hear how much more of the voice and piano comes through? Step 9 shows why.

Step 3 · What is sound?

Sound is air, wiggling

Something pushes the air, that air pushes the air next to it, and the push travels on until it reaches your ear. Play with it:

See the red dot? It's one bit of air, and it never travels: only the push does. Faster wiggles sound higher; bigger wiggles sound louder.

Step 4 · Drawing sound

Draw the wiggle, and you get a wave

Draw how the air moves over time and you get the familiar wave line. Keep this shape in mind: a gramophone record stores exactly this line, cut into its groove.

Real sounds are many wiggles added together. That's why a piano and a voice singing the same note still sound different.

Step 6 · How it makes sound

The groove is the sound

The groove is that wave line from step 4, cut into the record. The needle rides it and shakes a thin diaphragm, the diaphragm shakes the air, and the horn makes it loud enough to fill a room.

Slowed way down: a real needle wiggles hundreds of times a second.

Step 7 · Going electric

Swap the horn for electricity

A modern record player still has a groove and a needle. But the needle doesn't shake a diaphragm any more: it wiggles a tiny magnet inside a coil, and that makes electricity that wiggles the same way. In the speaker, a magnet turns it back into movement.

Slowed way down. The red and blue block is the tiny magnet on the needle.

Step 9 · It works backwards

A microphone is a speaker in reverse

Push the cone with sound, and the coil moves inside the magnet and makes a current. That's a microphone. It's how that 1925 recording got so much clearer: no more shouting into a horn.

Same parts as the speaker, used the other way round.

A spooky side note

Yes, your speakers can listen

Because a speaker and a microphone are the same parts, the only difference is which way the electricity flows. Spies know this too.

2016 · Headphones

Headphones that eavesdrop

Researchers at Ben-Gurion University wrote a program that switched a computer's headphone socket from "out" to "in". Plain headphones turned into a microphone and could pick up speech from across a room.

Read the research (SPEAKE(a)R)
2017 · Smart TVs

The TV that pretended to be off

Leaked CIA documents described "Weeping Angel": a hack that made some Samsung smart TVs fake being switched off while they recorded the room. That one used the TV's built-in microphone, the one meant for voice commands.

Step 10 · Cut out the needle

The speaker only needs the wiggle

Once the wiggle is electricity, the speaker doesn't care where it came from. Anything that can make that electrical wiggle can plug in and play music. Try it:

Step 11 · Sound files

A sound file is a list of numbers

Measure how high the wave is, again and again, very fast, and write each measurement down. That list of numbers is a WAV file. To play it, the phone turns each number back into a level of electricity, one after another, and the wiggle comes back.

song.wav label: numbers per second, how many ears 0, 64, -53, -30, 83, -33, -64, 87, 0, -91, 70, 38, -100, 38, 69, -88, 0, …

Few numbers give big, blocky steps. Real music uses 44,100 numbers every second for each ear, so the steps are far too small to hear.

Step 12 · Why MP3 won

WAV files are big

44,100 numbers every second, for each ear, adds up fast. An MP3 stores the same song with far fewer numbers, leaving out details your ears barely notice. Smaller files meant faster downloads and more songs in your pocket, so MP3 took over.

One 4-minute song On a 1999 modem
WAV 42 MB over 2 hours
MP3 4 MB about 13 minutes

Songs that fit on the first iPod (2001)

120 as WAV
1,000 as MP3

A note

How MP3 decides what to leave out is called compression, and that's a whole different story. We'll skip it here: for now, an MP3 is just a smaller list of numbers that turns back into the same wiggle.

Step 13 · Radio

Sound without a cable

A radio station turns sound into the same electrical wiggle, then sends it through the air as an invisible radio wave. Your radio's antenna catches it, turns it back into the electrical wiggle, and the speaker does the rest.

radio wave · sound

How radio waves work (and why every station has its own number on the dial) is another story. What matters here: the cable can be replaced by the air. Keep that in mind for Bluetooth.

Step 14 · Bluetooth

A tiny radio station in your phone

Bluetooth is radio again: very short-range, with its own kind of radio wave. But instead of the wiggle, it sends the numbers from the file. Inside the speaker, a little chip does what the headphone jack used to do: it turns the numbers back into the electrical wiggle.

radio · sound

Nothing here is new: the numbers from step 11, the radio from step 13, and the same coil, magnet and cone from step 8. And underneath it all, the gramophone's trick: read the wiggle, shake the air.
